### A2. Embedded Unit Map Example (Slide 2 image)

The deck shows a sample "Language" section from a district Unit Map:

- **Signature Practices:** CCD / Close Reading / Jigsaw / Notice & Wonder
- **Language Focus:** Students learn to use adjectives to describe people and abilities, talk about what people can do, ask and answer questions about skills and talents, and share opinions supported by evidence or examples.
- **Language Functions & Structures** (define, describe, compare, justify):
  1. *Defining/Explaining:* "A smart person is someone who ___." / "Intelligence means ___."
  2. *Describing abilities:* "He/She is good at ___." / "They can ___ very well."
  3. *Justifying opinions:* "I think ___ is smart because ___." / "___ shows intelligence when ___."
  4. *Comparing ideas:* "Both ___ and ___ are smart, but ___." / "___ is more ___ than ___."
  5. *Cause/effect:* "___ is smart because ___." / "If ___, then ___ shows intelligence."
- **Vocabulary.** Academic: analyze, complex, design, expert, focus, predict. Key: ability, cognitively, control, insensitive, intact, limitation, achievement, ambition, gauge, matter, personality, trait.

> This is a good "real-world artifact" to put in the game room: teachers can see that the Unit Map already gives them the **function** and **demand**, and they only need to add the **domain** to finish the formula.

### A3. Summary of the Deck

A language objective names **the specific language students will use to access and show their learning**. It sits next to the content objective: the content objective is the *what*, and the language objective is the *how*. It has a dual purpose: content access, and continued English development. Teachers build one in three moves, **Function (DO) → Demand (USE) → Domain (HOW)**, which gives the formula **"Students will [DO] + [USE] + [HOW]."** A strong objective meets 3 criteria: it connects to the task, names the language needed, and gives students real chances to use language. Teachers should write an exemplar student response first and work backward to see what language it takes, keeping a focal "MLL Heart Student" in mind. Objectives should be **visible** in the classroom (domain icons, cubes, "I can" charts). District curricula (Illustrative Math, Into Literature, Benchmark Advance) and the Unit Map "Language" section often provide part of the objective already.

**Core ideas, in order of difficulty:**
1. Definition and purpose (dual purpose)
2. Content objective vs. language objective
3. The 4 domains
4. Function vs. demand
5. The formula (DO + USE + HOW)
6. The 3 criteria (quality check)
7. Application: write your own, peer review, make it visible
8. Commitment

## PART B: Learning Objectives in California ELD (Research Summary)

### B1. The California Framework Behind Language Objectives

California's guidance comes from four main documents. Language objectives are how a teacher turns them into a daily lesson.

| Source | What it says that matters for objectives |
|---|---|
| **CA ELD Standards** (adopted by the State Board of Education, Nov 2012; published 2014) | Defines *what* language ELs should learn at each grade and proficiency level. These are the "language standards" a language objective should come from. |
| **CA ELA/ELD Framework** (2014) | Requires **Integrated ELD** (ELD standards used *together with* content standards in every subject) and **Designated ELD** (a protected time in the day where the ELD standards are the focus). Gives the lesson-planning questions below. |
| **CA English Learner Roadmap** (SBE policy, July 12, 2017) | Four principles: (1) Assets-oriented and needs-responsive schools; (2) **Intellectual quality of instruction and meaningful access**; (3) System conditions that support effectiveness; (4) Alignment and articulation within and across systems. Language objectives directly support Principle 2: grade-level content **with** language support, not watered-down content. |
| **ELPAC** (state English language proficiency test) | Tests the same **4 domains** the deck uses (Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing) and reports an Oral Language and a Written Language score. Overall performance levels 1–4. |

### B2. Structure of the CA ELD Standards (useful vocabulary for teachers)

**Part I: Interacting in Meaningful Ways**, organized by 3 **communicative modes** (12 strands):

- **A. Collaborative** (talking/writing *with* others): 1. Exchanging information and ideas · 2. Interacting via written English · 3. Offering opinions and negotiating/persuading · 4. Adapting language choices to context
- **B. Interpretive** (understanding texts and speech): 5. Listening actively · 6. Reading/viewing closely · 7. Evaluating language choices · 8. Analyzing language choices
- **C. Productive** (creating oral/written texts): 9. Presenting · 10. Writing · 11. Supporting opinions / justifying arguments · 12. Selecting and applying precise vocabulary and language resources

**Part II: Learning About How English Works** (the "demand" side):

- **A. Structuring Cohesive Texts:** text structure; cohesion
- **B. Expanding and Enriching Ideas:** verbs/verb phrases; nouns/noun phrases; modifying to add detail
- **C. Connecting and Condensing Ideas:** combining clauses; condensing ideas

**Part III: Using Foundational Literacy Skills**

**3 proficiency levels:** **Emerging → Expanding → Bridging**, with matching levels of support: **substantial → moderate → light**.

> **How this connects to the deck:**
> - The deck's **4 domains** = *how* students use language (and what ELPAC tests).
> - CA's **3 modes** (collaborative, interpretive, productive) = *the kind of interaction*. Listening/reading are mostly interpretive; speaking/writing are productive; partner talk and shared writing are collaborative.
> - The deck's **language functions** (explain, compare, justify…) match Part I strands and the standards' own list of purposes for using language: *"describing, entertaining, informing, interpreting, analyzing, recounting, explaining, persuading, negotiating, justifying, evaluating."*
> - The deck's **language demands** (noun phrases, complex sentences, past-tense verbs, connectives) are basically **Part II** of the CA ELD Standards.

### B3. CDE's "Framing Questions for Lesson Planning" (ELA/ELD Framework, Ch. 2, Fig. 2.16)

**For all students:**
- What are the big ideas and culminating performance tasks of the larger unit of study, and how does this lesson build toward them?
- **What are the learning targets for this lesson, and what should students be able to do at the end of the lesson?**
- Which clusters of CA CCSS for ELA/Literacy does this lesson address?
- What background knowledge, skills, and experiences do my students have related to this lesson?
- How complex are the texts and tasks?
- How will students make meaning, express themselves effectively, develop language, and learn content? How will they apply or learn foundational skills?
- What types of scaffolding, accommodations, or modifications will individual students need to engage effectively in the lesson tasks?
- How will my students and I monitor learning during and after the lesson, and how will that inform instruction?

**Add for English learners:**
- What are the English language proficiency levels of my students?
- **Which CA ELD Standards amplify the CA CCSS for ELA/Literacy at students' English language proficiency levels?**
- **What language might be new for students and/or present challenges?**
- **How will students interact in meaningful ways and learn about how English works in collaborative, interpretive, and/or productive modes?**

> The bold questions line up with the deck's formula: ELD standard → **function**; new or challenging language → **demand**; interaction mode → **domain/HOW**.

### B4. One Objective, Three Proficiency Levels (example from Grade 5)

CA ELD Standard **ELD.PI.5.5 (Listening actively)** shows how one skill shifts across levels:

- **Emerging:** ask and answer *basic* questions, *with prompting and substantial support*
- **Expanding:** ask and answer *detailed* questions, *with occasional prompting and moderate support*
- **Bridging:** ask and answer *detailed* questions, *with minimal prompting and light support*

**Applied to the deck's formula** (content: the American Revolution):

| Level | Language objective |
|---|---|
| Emerging | Students will **explain** one cause of the American Revolution **using a word bank and the frame "One cause was ___ because ___"** **by speaking with a partner.** |
| Expanding | Students will **explain** how the French and Indian War led to the American Revolution **using cause/effect connectives (because, as a result) and topic vocabulary** **by speaking with a partner and writing two sentences.** |
| Bridging | Students will **explain and justify** the connection between the French and Indian War and the American Revolution **using complex sentences, citation verbs ("The text states…"), and precise vocabulary** **in a partner discussion and a written paragraph.** |

The content objective stays the **same grade-level goal** for everyone (Roadmap Principle 2). The **support and the language demand** change.

### B5. California-Specific Takeaways for the PD

1. **Every teacher of ELs teaches ELD.** Integrated ELD happens in all content areas, so language objectives belong in math, science, and history too, not only ELA.
2. **Language objectives should come from the CA ELD Standards** at the students' proficiency levels, used "in tandem with" the content standards.
3. **Keep the content rigorous and adjust the language support** (substantial, moderate, or light) instead of lowering the content objective.
4. **Plan for interaction.** CA's standards are built on collaborative, interpretive, and productive modes, so a language objective should create real chances to talk, read, and write, not just fill in worksheets. This matches the deck's Criterion 3.
5. **Assets view.** The ELD Standards and the Roadmap treat home languages and cultures as resources. The deck's "4 + 1" visual (the +1 being translanguaging/metalinguistic connections) fits this.
6. **The domains match ELPAC.** Objectives that deliberately rotate through listening, speaking, reading, and writing build the skills the ELPAC measures.
7. **Policy update (verify with your district):** The CDE says **AB 2555** makes the Summative ELPAC the *sole* reclassification criterion (Overall Performance Level 4; Alternate ELPAC Overall PL 3). Teacher evaluation, parent consultation, and basic-skills measures are no longer criteria. The CDE lists an effective date of **Sept 27, 2026**, and eligible students must be reclassified by **Jan 31, 2027** based on 2025–26 scores. The CDE notes its FAQ guidance is still being updated.

### B6. Background (not from CDE)

Pairing content objectives with language objectives is popularized by the **SIOP Model** (Echevarría, Vogt & Short). The deck's approach (function + demand + domain) is consistent with SIOP and with CA's ELD Standards.
