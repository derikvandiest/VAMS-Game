# ELD PD Game: Room: Grading for Equity

**Topic:** Grading and testing with Multilingual Learners (MLLs) in mind
**Suggested campus level:** Mid-to-upper grade room. This topic builds on the Learning Objectives room (5th grade), because grading content and language separately requires knowing the difference between a content objective and a language objective.
**Sources:** No director deck for this one. Everything here comes from research (see the Sources section). Part A = the Grading for Equity framework · Part B = grading and testing MLLs · Part C = California rules and supports · Part D = the game room blueprint

## PART A: The "Grading for Equity" Framework (Joe Feldman)

### A1. Background

- **Book:** *Grading for Equity: What It Is, Why It Matters, and How It Can Transform Schools and Classrooms*, by Joe Feldman (Corwin). 1st edition 2019; **2nd edition 2023**, which updates several practices, including proficiency scales.
- **Organization:** Crescendo Education Group, Feldman's consulting group. It has worked with California districts such as Placer Union HSD (since 2016) and Santa Clara Unified.
- **Core argument:** Traditional grading mixes academic mastery with behavior, compliance, and circumstance. Because of that, grades often reflect a student's resources (time at home, a quiet place to work, English proficiency, understanding of "school rules") as much as their learning.

### A2. The Three Pillars

An equitable grade is:

| Pillar | Meaning | Guiding question for teachers |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Accurate** | The grade reflects **only what the student knows and can do** in relation to the standards, not behavior, effort, or timing. | "If I showed this grade to someone else, would it tell them what the student actually learned?" |
| **2. Bias-resistant** | The grade keeps out teacher subjectivity and factors tied to a student's background (race, income, language, home environment). | "Could this practice give two students with the same learning different grades?" |
| **3. Motivational** | Grading encourages students to keep learning, gives them hope and a chance to recover, and builds intrinsic motivation instead of compliance. | "Does this practice make a struggling student want to try again, or give up?" |

### A3. Core Equitable Grading Practices

| # | Practice | Traditional approach | Equitable alternative | Pillar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **No zeros / minimum grading** | A missed assignment = 0 on a 0–100 scale, which is mathematically almost impossible to recover from | Use a **0–4 scale**, a minimum score (e.g., 50), or "Incomplete." Gather evidence in another way | Accurate, Motivational |
| 2 | **Grade on summative evidence** | Homework and classwork count heavily in the grade | **Practice (homework/formative) gets feedback, not points**; the grade comes from summative evidence of mastery | Accurate, Bias-resistant |
| 3 | **Remove behavior from the grade** | Points for participation, effort, neatness, bringing supplies; late penalties | Grade **content mastery only**; report behaviors/"soft skills" separately | Accurate, Bias-resistant |
| 4 | **No extra credit** | Extra credit for bringing tissues, attending events, and so on | Remove it. It inflates grades with non-academic factors and rewards students with more resources | Accurate, Bias-resistant |
| 5 | **Retakes and redos** | One shot; maybe retakes capped at 70% | Allow reassessment once the student has done more learning; the **new score replaces the old one** | Motivational, Accurate |
| 6 | **Weight recent performance** | Averaging all scores across the semester | Emphasize the **most recent / strongest evidence** of mastery, since learning grows over time | Accurate |
| 7 | **Standards-based gradebook and rubrics** | Gradebook organized by assignment ("Quiz 3," "HW 12") | Organize by **standard/skill**; use clear rubrics and proficiency scales | Accurate, Bias-resistant |
| 8 | **No group grades** | A whole group shares one project grade | Grade each student's **individual** evidence of learning | Accurate |
| 9 | **Clear, transparent criteria** | The student doesn't know what "an A paper" means | Share success criteria and exemplars **before** the task | Bias-resistant, Motivational |

> **What the evidence shows:** The case for these practices rests mostly on mathematical arguments (e.g., the 0–100 scale's weighting toward failure), district reports, and teacher experience. Critics (*Education Next*) note that large-scale controlled outcome studies are limited. For the PD, present these as **research-informed practices with strong reasoning behind them**, not proven-at-scale interventions.

### A4. Why This Matters More for MLLs

Many traditional grading practices **penalize MLLs for language and circumstance instead of content learning**:
- **Participation points** reward students who speak up in English. A newcomer may understand fully but not yet produce oral English.
- **Homework grades** reward access to English-speaking help at home.
- **Neatness/spelling/grammar** points on a science or history task lower the *content* grade for *language* reasons.
- **Zeros for late or missing work** hit students who are still learning school routines, translating directions, or carrying family responsibilities.
- **One-shot tests** measure the student's English on test day as much as the content.

## PART B: Grading and Testing MLLs (Research Summary)

### B1. The Big Idea: Separate Language from Content

> **"Is this assessment measuring the content, or measuring the student's English?"**

The core rule across the research and practitioner literature: **a content grade should reflect content knowledge.** Language growth is real and important, but it should be **measured and reported separately**, against the ELD standards.

- **Dual-scale rubrics:** Score **content** (e.g., historical reasoning, math accuracy, scientific explanation) and **language** (e.g., use of academic vocabulary, sentence structure) on **two separate rows/scales**. A student can earn a high content score and a developing language score on the same task.
- **Feedback vs. grade:** Give feedback on English, but **let the content grade reflect understanding of the content.**
- **This connects directly to the Learning Objectives room:** content objective → content grade; language objective → language feedback/ELD progress.

### B2. Construct-Relevant vs. Construct-Irrelevant Language

- **Construct** = the skill the assessment is *supposed* to measure.
- **Construct-irrelevant language** = complex wording, idioms, long sentences, or unfamiliar cultural references that make an item harder **without being part of what's tested**.
- **Research base (Jamal Abedi and colleagues):**
  - Abedi & Lord (2001) linguistically simplified released NAEP math items. ELLs and students in lower-level math classes scored higher on the simplified versions, even though the math stayed the same.
  - Abedi's work identifies dozens of linguistic features (e.g., passive voice, long noun phrases, conditional clauses, unfamiliar words) that can be simplified **without changing what the item measures**.
  - A federal IES/WestEd study (2010) found that linguistically modified math items helped ELLs focus on and grasp the math concepts.
  - Caveat: research on ELL accommodations overall is mixed. Linguistic simplification is one of the more promising ones.
- **California's own framework cites this research.** The ELA/ELD Framework (Ch. 8) warns that biased assessments "may underestimate the students' achievement or reflect abilities that are not related to the assessment's content" (citing Abedi & Lord 2001).

**Teacher checklist for writing test items:**
- [ ] Short, direct sentences; active voice
- [ ] Familiar, everyday words for non-content vocabulary (keep the *content* vocabulary being tested)
- [ ] No idioms or culturally specific references unless they are the content
- [ ] Visuals, diagrams, and labeled examples
- [ ] Directions in numbered steps; read aloud or translated if needed
- [ ] Ask: "Would a fluent English speaker who knows this content get it right? Would an MLL who knows this content?"

### B3. Accommodation vs. Modification (Jung & Guskey Inclusive Grading Model)

Lee Ann Jung & Thomas Guskey (*Educational Leadership*, 2010; *Grading Exceptional and Struggling Learners*, Corwin) give a model for grading students with disabilities **and English learners**:

| | **Accommodation** | **Modification** |
|---|---|---|
| What changes? | **How** the student accesses the work or shows mastery | **What** the student is expected to learn (the standard itself) |
| Standard | Same grade-level standard | Different/adjusted standard |
| Examples for MLLs | Extra time, bilingual glossary, read-aloud directions, oral response, sentence frames, word bank, simplified wording | Using a lower-grade vocabulary standard, a shortened content expectation |
| Grading impact | **No change to grading.** Grade against the grade-level standard | Grade against the modified standard **and say so transparently** (report card/transcript note) |

- **Context matters:** the same support can be an accommodation in one task and a modification in another. Extra time is an accommodation on a test of understanding but a **modification** on a test of speed/fluency.
- **For most MLLs, the goal is accommodations, not modifications.** Keep the grade-level content and change the access. This matches the CA EL Roadmap principle of intellectual rigor with meaningful access.

### B4. Let Students Show What They Know (Multiple Modalities)

Research-informed options for MLLs to demonstrate **content** mastery:
- **Oral responses / conferences** in place of, or alongside, written answers
- **Labeled diagrams, drawings, graphic organizers, models**
- **Demonstrations, performances, hands-on tasks**
- **Portfolios** showing growth over time
- **Primary language responses** (when the goal is content, and someone can read them)
- **Sentence frames and word banks** (an accommodation, since the content stays the same)
- **Bilingual glossaries / dictionaries**, **extended time**, **read-aloud of directions**

### B5. Formative Assessment with MLLs (CA ELA/ELD Framework, Ch. 8)

- Teachers should **"take great care"** with formative decisions for ELs: a student who can't fully express ideas orally in a class discussion **may still understand the topic**.
- All ELs, at every proficiency level, can engage in **intellectually rich tasks at the same cognitive level** as English-proficient peers.
- **Primary language strategy:** newcomers (Emerging level) can **write a quick response in their primary language first, then in English**. Comparing the two shows what the student *knows* vs. what they can *express in English*. Teachers who don't speak the language can work with EL specialists, colleagues, or community members.
- Use quick-response tech (mini whiteboards, polls) to check understanding without relying only on extended English output.

### B6. Grade Expectations by Proficiency Level (CA ELD Proficiency Level Descriptors)

Use these to set **realistic language expectations** on the *language* row of a rubric. They are not a reason to lower content expectations.

| Level | Support needed | What students can typically do (summarized from the CA PLDs) |
|---|---|---|
| **Emerging** | Substantial | Respond with gestures, words, or short phrases; express ideas using **visuals, drawings, charts, graphic organizers**; read very brief text supported by pictures |
| **Expanding** | Moderate | Express ideas and opinions in short-to-extended sentences; take part in structured academic conversations; write expanded responses in context |
| **Bridging** | Light | Express complex ideas using extended discourse; take part fully in grade-level conversations with occasional support; read increasingly complex grade-level text |

> **Example: 6th grade science explanation (Emerging student):** A labeled diagram plus a 2-sentence frame ("The ___ causes ___ because ___") that shows the correct cause-and-effect = **proficient on content**. The language row notes "Emerging, producing learned phrases with support."

## PART C: California-Specific Rules and Supports

### C1. Who Decides the Grade: Education Code §49066

- In California, the **grade given to each pupil is the grade determined by the teacher** of the course. Absent clerical or mechanical mistake, fraud, bad faith, or incompetency, that grade is **final**. Parents can request a grade change only on those grounds.
- **For the PD:** teachers have real authority, and real responsibility, to make grades **accurate**. Grading practices are a teacher-controlled equity lever. Local district/board grading policies still apply.

### C2. Two Separate Reports: Content Grades and ELD Progress

- Many California districts report **ELD progress separately** from content grades. LAUSD's elementary progress report, for example, gives ELs separate ELD marks for each of the **3 modes of Part I** (Collaborative, Interpretive, Productive) and the **3 processes of Part II** of the CA ELD Standards.
- LAUSD's marking guidance has teachers evaluate students on progress toward **grade-level standards and, for ELs, English language proficiency standards**. These are two separate things, which reinforces "separate language from content."
- **Check your district's report card:** does it have an ELD section? If so, that is where language progress belongs, not inside the science or math grade.

### C3. California Assessment Principles (ELA/ELD Framework, Ch. 8)

Assessments must be **valid, reliable, and free from bias**:
- **Valid:** measures what it claims to measure. "No test is valid for all purposes."
- **Reliable:** consistent results across time and scorers. Necessary but **not sufficient** for validity.
- **Free from bias:** "produces the same scores for students of the same attainment level, irrespective of their demographic subgroup," **including primary language**.
- Popham's **two forms of bias**: (1) *Offensiveness*: content that upsets or stereotypes a group. (2) *Unfair penalization*: content that is harder for some students for reasons unrelated to what's tested, such as unfamiliar cultural vocabulary, images, or **language demands beyond the targeted students' level**.

### C4. State Testing Supports for ELs (CAASPP / Smarter Balanced, 2025–26)

California's accessibility system has three tiers: **universal tools** (everyone), **designated supports** (for students with an identified need, including ELs, decided by educators), and **accommodations** (IEP/504).
Designated supports commonly used by ELs include:
- **Translated glossaries (math):** 14 languages, including Spanish, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Korean, Arabic, Cantonese, Hmong, and others; covers selected construct-irrelevant terms
- **Translated glossaries (science):** 10 languages
- **Stacked / dual-language translations and translated test directions in Spanish** (renamed in the 2026–27 matrix)
- **Text-to-speech in Spanish** (science)
- Universal tools such as the **English glossary**

> **For the PD:** if students use glossaries, translated directions, and read-aloud on the state test, **they should be practicing with those same supports on classroom assessments.** Check the current year's *CA Accessibility Resources Matrix* for exact eligibility.

### C5. ELPAC and Reclassification (Update)

- The **ELPAC** measures the 4 domains (Listening, Speaking, Reading, Writing). It is the English-proficiency measure. **Classroom content grades should not be acting as a second English test.**
- **AB 2555 (CDE guidance):** the Summative ELPAC (Overall Performance Level 4; Alternate ELPAC Overall PL 3) is now the **sole reclassification criterion**. Teacher evaluation, parent consultation, and the basic-skills comparison are no longer criteria. The CDE lists an effective date of **Sept 27, 2026**, and eligible students must be reclassified by **Jan 31, 2027** based on 2025–26 scores. *Verify locally, because the CDE notes its FAQ is still being updated.*
- **Why it matters for grading:** under the old system, a low content grade could block reclassification. That is no longer true, but grades still drive course placement, A–G eligibility, GPA, and student self-concept, so accurate grades matter just as much.

### C6. Alignment with the CA EL Roadmap

- **Principle 1, Assets-oriented:** value primary language (e.g., primary-language responses as evidence of content knowledge).
- **Principle 2, Intellectual quality and meaningful access:** keep grade-level content and use accommodations, not watered-down standards.
- Equitable grading puts both principles into the gradebook.

## PART D: Game Blueprint: The "Grading for Equity" Classroom

Participants enter, explore stations in any order (with a suggested path), and earn a badge after the final challenge.

| Station (in-room object) | Content source | What the participant does | Mechanic idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The Report Card on the Wall** (hook) | A4 | Sees two students' grade histories: same learning, different grades | **"Spot the difference":** reveal that one student lost points for participation, a late assignment, and spelling on a science test |
| **2. The Three Pillars Podium** | A2 | Learns Accurate / Bias-resistant / Motivational | Click each pillar → definition + guiding question; quick match quiz |
| **3. The Gradebook Desk** | A3 | Sorts grading practices | **Drag-and-drop:** sort 10 practices into "Equitable" / "Traditional"; feedback explains each |
| **4. The Zero Calculator** | A3 #1 | Experiences the math of zeros | **Interactive calculator:** add one zero to a row of 85s on a 0–100 scale vs. a 0–4 scale and compare the results |
| **5. The Two-Lens Microscope** | B1 | Separates language from content | Look at a student work sample through a "Content lens" and a "Language lens"; score each on a dual rubric |
| **6. The Test Item Repair Shop** | B2, C3 | Removes construct-irrelevant language | **Fix-it game:** rewrite a wordy math or science item using the checklist; compare with an expert rewrite |
| **7. The Accommodation or Modification? Booth** | B3 | Classifies supports | **Card flip:** "Extra time on a reading-comprehension test": accommodation or modification? Explain why |
| **8. The Show-What-You-Know Gallery** | B4, B6 | Matches assessment options to proficiency levels | Match an Emerging/Expanding/Bridging student profile to fitting response options |
| **9. The California Law Library** | C1–C5 | Learns Ed Code 49066, CAASPP supports, ELPAC/AB 2555 | **Scavenger hunt:** find 4 "facts" hidden on bookshelves to unlock the final challenge |
| **10. Final Challenge: "Regrade It"** | All | Re-grades a real-feeling MLL assignment | Given a traditionally graded MLL assignment (participation points, spelling deductions, a late penalty), participant **rebuilds the grade** using a dual rubric, then justifies each change with a pillar |
| **11. Exit Door: Commitment** | – | One grading practice to change | Private note + public post on the campus board ("One practice I'll change in my gradebook is…") |

**Room badge:** "Equity Grader" (or similar), earned after Stations 6 and 10.
**Carry-forward item:** a **dual-scale rubric template** (content row + language row) added to inventory. It pairs with the Learning Objectives room's formula card (content objective → content row; language objective → language row).

**Possible connections to other rooms:**
- Learning Objectives (5th grade): content vs. language objectives feed the two rubric rows
- Future rooms: Scaffolds/Sentence Frames, Formative Assessment, Integrated vs. Designated ELD, ELPAC Data

## Quick-Reference Card (for participants)

1. **Grade the content, coach the language.** Use two rows on the rubric.
2. **Ask: is this measuring the content or the English?** Remove construct-irrelevant language from test items.
3. **Accommodate, don't modify.** Same standard, different access.
4. **No zeros, no behavior points, no extra credit.** Grades = learning.
5. **Let them try again.** The new evidence replaces the old.
6. **Practice with the supports they'll get on CAASPP** (glossaries, translated directions).
7. **Primary language counts as evidence** of content knowledge.
8. **You decide the grade (Ed Code 49066)**, so make it accurate.
