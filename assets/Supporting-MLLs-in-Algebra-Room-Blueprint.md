# ELD PD Game: Room: Supporting Our MLLs in Math (Algebra)

**Topic:** Making algebra accessible and rigorous for Multilingual Learners (MLLs)
**Suggested campus level:** Upper grade room (8th/9th, where Algebra I lives). It builds on two earlier rooms:

- **Grading for Equity:** grading the math, not the English

## PART A: Why Language Matters in Algebra

### A1. The Data

California CAASPP Math, **% of English Learners meeting or exceeding standard** (CA Mathematics Framework, Fig. 2.1):

| 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2021 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11% | 12% | 12% | 13% | 13% | 8% | 10% |

(For comparison: all Hispanic/Latino students were at 28% in 2019 and 21% in 2022.)

- **Access to Algebra:** research consistently finds unequal access to accelerated math for ELs. For example, a Virginia IES study found **42% of English learners did not take** the accelerated pathway leading to middle school Algebra I. California's **Math Placement Act (2015)** was passed partly in response to inequities in placement for Black, Latino, low-income, and EL students.
- **Recent reporting (Education Week, May 2026):** for secondary ELs, **language, more than computation,** is often the main barrier in math class.

### A2. Algebra Is a Language Inside a Language

Algebra asks MLLs to work in **three languages at once**: everyday English, academic math English, and **symbolic notation**. Common trouble spots:

**1. Multiple-meaning words** (everyday meaning ≠ math meaning)

| Word | Everyday meaning | Algebra meaning |
|---|---|---|
| term | a school semester; a word | a part of an expression separated by + or − |
| expression | a look on your face; a saying | a combination of numbers, variables, and operations (no "=") |
| power | strength, electricity | an exponent / repeated multiplication |
| product | something sold in a store | the result of multiplying |
| root | the part of a plant | a solution of an equation / zero of a function |
| solution | a liquid mixture; an answer to a problem | the value(s) that make an equation true |
| table | furniture | an organized display of x- and y-values |
| like (terms) | to enjoy | terms with the same variable and exponent |
| degree | temperature; a college diploma | the highest exponent of a polynomial |
| function | an event; how something works | a rule assigning exactly one output to each input |
| slope | a hill | rate of change (rise/run) |
| odd | strange | not divisible by 2 |

**2. Word-order traps** (English order ≠ symbolic order)
- "5 **less than** x" → **x − 5** (not 5 − x)
- "**Subtract** 4 **from** y" → **y − 4**
- "The **quotient of** x **and** 3" → **x ÷ 3**
- "x **exceeds** y **by** 3" → **x = y + 3**
- "Twice the **sum of** a number and 4" → **2(n + 4)** (not 2n + 4)

**3. The "Reversal Error"** (Clement and colleagues; widely replicated)
- *"There are 6 times as many students as professors."* Many students (including fluent English speakers and college students) write **6S = P** instead of **S = 6P**.
- **Causes:** **word-order matching** (copying English order into symbols) and a **static-comparison** view of the variable (S = "students" as a label instead of "the *number* of students").
- **Language link:** a bilingual study (Basque/Spanish) found error rates changed depending on the **language** the problem was given in, so grammar and word order matter.
- **Classroom fixes:**
  - Define variables as **quantities**: "Let *s* = the **number** of students"
  - **Test with numbers**: "If there are 10 professors, how many students are there? Does your equation give 60?"
  - Use **tables** or **tape diagrams** before writing the equation
  - Use **neutral variable letters** (x, y) instead of first letters (S, P), which can trigger the label error

**4. Dense, compressed language**
Word problems pack conditional clauses ("If…, then…"), passive voice ("The cost is determined by…"), and long noun phrases ("the total cost of renting the bike for h hours"). Abedi's research (see the Grading for Equity room) shows this **construct-irrelevant** language lowers EL scores without measuring algebra.

**5. Different math conventions from other countries**
- Many countries use a **decimal comma**: 3,5 = 3.5. Periods or spaces separate thousands.
- In Spanish, **billón = 10¹²** (a trillion in English).
- Division may be written differently. Latin America often uses a different long-division layout, and "÷" vs. "/" vs. a fraction bar may be unfamiliar.
- Multiplication may be shown with **·** or **×**, so "x" as a variable can confuse.
- **Treat these as assets.** Ask students to show how they learned it, and connect the two methods.

### A3. Research Principles: Language as a Resource (Judit Moschkovich)

Moschkovich's research (cited throughout the CA Math Framework):
- Treat students' language, **including their home language, gestures, and drawings**, as a **resource, not a deficit**.
- The "language of math" is **much more than vocabulary**. It is the ability to **explain, justify, argue, and make sense** of math with others.
- Focus on **students' mathematical reasoning, not on correcting their English**.
- **Code-switching/translanguaging** is a sophisticated communication strategy, not a gap.
- MLLs **don't need to master English before doing rigorous math.** They learn language **through** meaningful math talk.

### A4. Retire the "Key Words" Strategy

Teaching "**'total' means add**", "**'less' means subtract**", or "**'of' means multiply**" seems helpful for MLLs but backfires:
- "Maria has 12 apples. She has 4 **more** than Juan. How many does Juan have?" → the "key word" says add, but the answer is 12 − 4.
- Keyword lists **skip sense-making**, which is exactly what MLLs need most. Math educators list this among the "rules that expire" (Karp, Bush & Dougherty, 2014).
- **Instead:** use **Three Reads** (Part B), visual models, and "act it out" to understand the *situation* first.

## PART B: Stanford's Mathematical Language Routines (MLRs)

**Source:** *Principles for the Design of Mathematics Curricula: Promoting Language and Content Development* (Zwiers, Dieckmann, Rutherford-Quach, Daro, Skarin, Weiss & Malamut; Understanding Language/SCALE, Stanford, 2017; CC-BY).
**Why this matters for us:** **Illustrative Mathematics, our district curriculum, builds these exact routines into its lessons** (look for "MLR" labels in the teacher guide's "Access for English Learners" notes).

### B1. The 4 Design Principles

| # | Principle | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Support sense-making** | Scaffold tasks and **amplify** language so students can make their own meaning. *Amplify, don't simplify*: add visuals, context, and multiple ways in instead of removing the challenging math or text. |
| 2 | **Optimize output** | Give students many chances to **produce** math language (speaking, writing, drawing) with purpose. |
| 3 | **Cultivate conversation** | Structure pair, small-group, and whole-class math discussions so students **must** talk. |
| 4 | **Maximize linguistic and cognitive meta-awareness** | Help students notice *how* math language works and reflect on their own thinking. |

### B2. The 8 Math Language Routines (with Algebra Examples)

| MLR | Purpose | Algebra I example |
|---|---|---|
| **MLR1: Stronger and Clearer Each Time** | Students write a response, refine it through several structured partner conversations, then rewrite it | "Explain why 2(x + 3) and 2x + 6 are equivalent." Pre-write → 3 partner rotations (partners ask "Why did you…?") → post-write that shows clearer, more precise language |
| **MLR2: Collect and Display** | Teacher scribes student language onto a shared display, then connects it to formal terms | While students talk about a graph, record "it goes up 3 every time" → connect it to "**rate of change** / **slope** = 3." Keep it on the wall all unit |
| **MLR3: Critique, Correct, and Clarify** | Students fix a flawed or ambiguous worked example | Show "6S = P" for the students/professors problem, or a solution that "forgot to distribute the negative." Students find the error and write a clearer explanation |
| **MLR4: Information Gap** | Partners hold different information and *must* communicate | Partner A: "How long until the tank is empty?" Partner B's data card: starting volume, drain rate. A must ask for what's needed and justify why |
| **MLR5: Co-Craft Questions and Problems** | Students generate the math questions before seeing the real one | Show a situation: "A phone plan costs $20 a month plus $0.10 per text." Students write the questions they could ask, then the teacher reveals the task |
| **MLR6: Three Reads** | Read a problem 3 times with a different focus each time | **Read 1:** What is the situation? (no numbers). **Read 2:** What quantities and relationships are there? **Read 3:** What are some ways to solve it? *(See the Values/Units chart in C2)* |
| **MLR7: Compare and Connect** | Compare different representations and strategies | Students compare a **table, graph, and equation** for the same situation: "Where is the $20 in each one? Where is the $0.10?" Also: "Which One Doesn't Belong?" with 4 equations or graphs |
| **MLR8: Discussion Supports** | Moves that support rich discussion | **Revoice** ("So you're saying the y-intercept is the starting amount?"), **press for details**, sentence frames, **choral practice** of new terms, think-alouds, gestures, acting out |

**Light vs. heavy supports:** The Stanford framework tells teachers to choose "heavier" or "lighter" supports based on the language demands of the task **and** students' English proficiency (Emerging / Expanding / Bridging).

## PART C: Algebra-Specific Strategies for MLLs

### C1. Multiple Representations (Connect All Four)

```
        VERBAL / CONTEXT
   "Phone plan: $20 + $0.10 per text"
            /            \
      TABLE                EQUATION
   x | y                  y = 0.10x + 20
   0 | 20                     \
  10 | 21                    GRAPH
  20 | 22          (line starting at 20, rising 1 per 10 texts)
```
- Every representation is a way in for MLLs: **tables, graphs, and symbols reduce the language load** while keeping the math rigorous.
- **Manipulatives and visuals:** algebra tiles, balance scales (for solving equations), tape diagrams, number lines, Desmos.
- Always connect back to **words** (MLR7 Compare and Connect): "What does the 20 **mean** in the situation?"

### C2. Three Reads + Values/Units Chart (from Stanford MLR6)

> *"It costs $3 per person to go to the zoo. Alexandra's family has a coupon for a $5 discount. There are p people in Alexandra's family. Write an expression for how much it would cost for them to go to the zoo."*

| Value | Units / meaning in context |
|---|---|
| 3 | $ per person to go to the zoo |
| 5 | $ discounted from the cost |
| p | number of people in the family |
| C | cost for the family to go to the zoo |

→ cost = ($ per person) × (number of people) − ($ discount) → **C = 3p − 5**

This turns a dense English sentence into structure **before** symbols, and it prevents the reversal error.

### C3. Spanish–English Cognates in Algebra (Spanish is CA's largest EL language)

| English | Spanish | | English | Spanish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| equation | ecuación | | function | función |
| expression | expresión | | linear | lineal |
| variable | variable | | quadratic | cuadrática |
| coefficient | coeficiente | | system | sistema |
| constant | constante | | solution | solución |
| term | término | | polynomial | polinomio |
| exponent | exponente | | intersection | intersección |
| distributive property | propiedad distributiva | | graph | gráfica |
| factor | factor | | quotient | cociente |
| product | producto | | value | valor |

**Not cognates (teach directly):** slope = *pendiente*, inequality = *desigualdad*, root = *raíz*, y-intercept = *ordenada al origen* / *intersección con el eje y*.
**Strategy:** add a cognate column to the word wall (MLR2) and ask, "Does this word look like a word you know in another language?"

### C4. Language Frames for Algebra (by Function)

| Language function | Emerging (substantial support) | Expanding (moderate) | Bridging (light) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Describe** a pattern | "It goes up ___ each time." | "Each time x increases by 1, y ___ by ___." | "The relationship is linear because the rate of change is constant at ___." |
| **Explain** a step | "First I ___. Then I ___." | "I ___ both sides by ___ so that ___." | "To isolate the variable, I applied the ___ property, which ___." |
| **Interpret** a parameter | "The ___ is the start." | "The y-intercept, ___, means ___." | "In this context, the slope of ___ represents ___ per ___." |
| **Justify** | "I think ___ because ___." | "This is correct because when I substitute ___, ___." | "This solution is valid because ___; however, ___ would not work since ___." |
| **Compare** | "___ and ___ are the same / different." | "Both ___ and ___ ___, but ___." | "Although ___ and ___ are equivalent, ___ is more efficient because ___." |
| **Critique** (SMP 3) | "I agree / disagree with ___." | "I disagree with ___'s step ___ because ___." | "___'s reasoning is valid up to ___, but the error occurs when ___." |

### C5. Writing a Language Objective for Algebra (from the Learning Objectives Room)

Formula: **Students will [DO] + [USE] + [HOW]**
- *Content objective:* Students will write a linear equation from a context.
- *Language objective:* Students will **interpret** the slope and y-intercept in context **using the frame "The ___ represents ___ per ___" and the terms *rate of change* and *starting value*** **by explaining to a partner and writing one sentence.**

### C6. Assessing Algebra Fairly (from the Grading for Equity Room)
- **Grade the algebra, not the English.** A correct equation, table, or graph with imperfect written English = proficient math.
- Use a **two-row rubric**: math reasoning row + math communication row.
- **Simplify construct-irrelevant language** in test items, but keep the algebra vocabulary that is being assessed.
- Let students **explain orally, annotate, or show work in their home language**.
- On CAASPP math, eligible ELs can use **translated glossaries (14 languages)** and **Spanish stacked translations**. Practice with them in class.

### C7. Teacher Moves Checklist
- [ ] Post a word wall with visuals, examples, and cognates (MLR2)
- [ ] Use Three Reads on every word problem (MLR6)
- [ ] Connect at least 2 representations each lesson (MLR7)
- [ ] Build in structured partner talk with frames (MLR1, MLR8)
- [ ] Revoice student ideas in precise math language; don't correct their English
- [ ] Welcome home language, gestures, and drawings as evidence of thinking
- [ ] Define variables as quantities ("the **number** of…")
- [ ] Avoid key-word tricks. Make sense of the situation first
- [ ] Use the "MLR" and "Access for English Learners" notes in Illustrative Math

## PART D: California Framework and Policy

### D1. 2023 California Mathematics Framework (adopted by the SBE in 2023; published 2024)
- **Rooted in the CA ELD Standards:** "Instruction for linguistically and culturally diverse English learners who are developing mathematical proficiency should be rooted in and informed by the California English Language Development Standards." *(Ch. 3)*
- **Focus on ideas, not vocabulary corrections:** teachers should listen for the math ideas students express, **noticing translanguaging and extralinguistic communication (gesture, representation)**, and "attend to students' mathematical ideas rather than focusing on correcting vocabulary." *(Ch. 2, citing Moschkovich)*
- **Revoicing** keeps discussion mathematical and lets the student confirm or correct the teacher's interpretation. *(Ch. 2)*
- **Open tasks** let students show thinking "numerically, symbolically, verbally, visually, or through physical action," which aligns with the ELD Standards and **Universal Design for Learning (UDL)**. *(Ch. 2)*
- **Peer interaction, multiple approaches, and multiple representations** benefit all students, including ELs. *(Ch. 3)*
- **Integrated ELD** in math: the framework pairs math content and Standards for Mathematical Practice with **ELD Part I: Interacting in Meaningful Ways** (e.g., collaborative discussion prompts such as "What does this symbol, the equal sign, mean to you?").
- **The framework's own classroom snapshots:**
  - *Ernesto (grades 4–5):* the teacher invites an EL to explain his strategy in Spanish and positions him as a mathematical contributor. Classmates compare his method to theirs.
  - *Lori (high school):* for **long-term English learners (LTELs)** who are fluent socially but challenged by disciplinary literacy, she posts **labeled images** and an **optional sentence frame** ("The fence should be arranged in a ___ shape because ___") to support rigorous problem solving.
- **Asset-based shift:** advocates (e.g., LMU's CEEL) note that the framework moves districts **away from remediation** and toward **grade-level access with scaffolding**, with integrated ELD in math.

### D2. ELD Standards ↔ Math Practices
| CA ELD Standards (Part I) | Standards for Mathematical Practice |
|---|---|
| 1. Exchanging information/ideas | SMP 3: Construct viable arguments / critique reasoning |
| 3. Offering and supporting opinions | SMP 3 |
| 6. Reading/viewing closely | SMP 1: Make sense of problems |
| 11. Justifying/arguing | SMP 3 |
| 12. Selecting precise vocabulary | SMP 6: Attend to precision |
| Part II: Connecting and condensing ideas | SMP 2: Reason abstractly and quantitatively (translating words ↔ symbols) |

### D3. California Mathematics Placement Act of 2015 (SB 359, Ed Code §51224.7)
- Districts serving grade 9 must have a **fair, objective, transparent math placement policy**, posted online.
- Placement must use **multiple objective measures** (state assessments, grades, diagnostic placement tests), not subjective impressions.
- It was motivated by documented inequities in access to advanced math for **Black, Latino, low-income, and English learner** students.
- **For the PD:** English proficiency alone should never keep a student out of Algebra I or an accelerated pathway.

### D4. State Testing Supports (CAASPP Math, 2025–26)
- **Translated glossaries in 14 languages** (Spanish, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Korean, Arabic, Cantonese, Hmong, and more) for selected construct-irrelevant terms
- **Spanish stacked / dual-language translations** and translated test directions
- Check the current CA Accessibility Resources Matrix for eligibility

## PART E: Game Blueprint: The "Algebra for MLLs" Classroom

| Station (in-room object) | Content source | What the participant does | Mechanic idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The Data Board** (hook) | A1 | Sees the EL math data and the access gap | Animated chart: guess the EL % meeting standard, then reveal it |
| **2. The Translation Station** | A2 | Experiences the language load of algebra | **"Lost in translation":** read a word problem in an unfamiliar language with only symbols in English, then reflect on how it felt |
| **3. The Multiple-Meaning Mirror** | A2 #1 | Matches everyday vs. algebra meanings | **Flip cards:** "term," "power," "root," "table." Flip to see the math meaning |
| **4. The Word-Order Workshop** | A2 #2–3 | Translates English into symbols | **Drag-and-drop:** build "5 less than x" correctly; solve the **students/professors** problem and see the reversal error explained |
| **5. The Key Word Trap** | A4 | Shows why key words fail | Given a key-word chart, solve 3 "trick" problems where the key word misleads, then pick a better strategy |
| **6. The MLR Toolbox** | B1–B2 | Learns the 4 principles and 8 routines | **Toolbox:** click each tool (MLR1–8), watch a short example, then match a routine to a classroom scenario |
| **7. The Three Reads Lab** | B2, C2 | Practices Three Reads + Values/Units | Guided 3-pass read of a word problem, filling in the Values/Units chart to build the equation |
| **8. The Representation Wall** | C1, MLR7 | Connects table, graph, equation, context | **Match-4 puzzle:** link the 4 representations of the same situation; answer "Where is the 20 in each?" |
| **9. The Cognate Café** | C3 | Learns Spanish–English cognates and non-cognates | **Memory game:** pair English and Spanish terms; flag the "not a cognate" cards |
| **10. The Frame Factory** | C4, C5 | Writes frames and a language objective | Choose a function (explain, justify, interpret) and a proficiency level → build a sentence frame; write a language objective with the [DO]+[USE]+[HOW] formula |
| **11. The CA Framework Library** | D1–D4 | Learns CMF guidance, Placement Act, CAASPP supports | **Scavenger hunt:** read the Ernesto and Lori snapshots; find 3 facts to unlock the final challenge |
| **12. Final Challenge: "Redesign the Lesson"** | All | Upgrades a traditional algebra lesson | Given a "worksheet-only" lesson (key words, no talk, no visuals), add **at least 3 MLRs, 1 representation connection, 1 language objective, and frames for 2 proficiency levels**; self-check with the C7 checklist |
| **13. Exit Door: Commitment** | – | One routine to try this week | Private note + public post ("This week I'll try MLR ___ in my ___ lesson") |

**Room badge:** "Algebra Language Champion" (or similar), earned after Stations 7 and 12.
**Carry-forward items:** an **MLR quick-reference card**, the **Values/Units chart template**, and the **algebra sentence frame bank**.
**Connections to earlier rooms:** the Learning Objectives formula card (used at Station 10) and the Grading for Equity dual-scale rubric (applied to algebra explanations).

## Quick-Reference Card (for participants)

1. **Algebra is a language.** Teach the words, the word order, and the symbols explicitly.
2. **Amplify, don't simplify.** Keep the rigor and add visuals, context, and representations.
3. **Three Reads before solving.** Situation → quantities → strategies.
4. **No key-word tricks.** Make sense of the situation first.
5. **Variables are numbers of things,** not labels. Test equations with real numbers.
6. **Talk every day.** Use structured partner talk with frames (MLR1, MLR8).
7. **Connect representations.** "Where is the ___ in the table, graph, and equation?"
8. **Home language, gestures, and drawings are math thinking.** Listen for ideas, not grammar.
9. **Use IM's built-in MLRs** and "Access for English Learners" notes.
10. **English proficiency is never a reason to deny Algebra access.**
